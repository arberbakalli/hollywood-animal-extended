# Hollywood Animal: Ultimate Early-Game Studio & Production Master Guide

This guide is a compilation of data-driven formulas, community-tested mechanics, and strategic decisions designed to optimize early-game performance in *Hollywood Animal*. It covers everything from script assembly and department hiring to theater allocation math and crisis management.

---

## 1. Pre-Production & Script Assembly

The foundation of your studio's profitability lies in how efficiently you structure your scripts before production even begins. In the early game, high-budget epics will bankrupt you; your goal is low-cost, high-return efficiency.

### 1.1 The "Holy Trinity" of Early-Game Genres
In Act 1, stick exclusively to **Comedy, Romance, and Drama** (and hybrids like Rom-Coms).
* **Why:** These genres rely on emotional beats and character interactions rather than expensive set pieces, stunts, or special effects.
* **What to Avoid:** Avoid Action, Sci-Fi, and Historical epics early on. They require expensive research tiers, elaborate sets, and massive production budgets that your early-game cash flow cannot support.

### 1.2 The "Storyboard Trick" for Writers
Never assign your starting writers to a real, expensive film script while their stats are low.
* **The Trick:** Have your writers churn out cheap 1-element or 2-element "Storyboard" scripts and immediately scrap them.
* **The Benefit:** This power-levels your writers' stats essentially for free. When you are ready to write your first *real* film, your writers will produce scripts that safely sit in the **3.5 to 5.0 score range**, preventing early box-office bombs.

### 1.3 Early Game Antagonists (No Crime Tech Required)
If you haven't unlocked heavy crime/violence nodes (like *Criminal Mastermind* or *Serial Killer*), use these cheap, penalty-free antagonist substitutes:
* **Bandit:** Safe, standard, and cheap. Perfect for light comedies or adventures.
* **Strict Parent / Annoying Suitor:** Excellent for Romance and Rom-Coms. They act as social obstacles rather than violent criminals, keeping the film family/teen-friendly.
* **Old Friend Turned Enemy:** Great for Drama. Adds emotional weight to a *Modern American Town* setting without needing high-tech sets.

### 1.4 Proven Early-Game Configurations
**The 5/6-Element Comedy (Low Budget / Family Appeal)**
* **Genre:** Comedy
* **Setting:** Modern American Town (Cheap to shoot)
* **Protagonist:** Clumsy Oaf
* **Antagonist:** Bandit (or Strict Parent)
* **Supporting:** Sidekick
* **Finale:** Antagonist Gets Punished

**The 5/6-Element Drama/Romance (*Rotten Fates* Build)**
* **Genre:** Drama (60%), Romance (30%), Thriller (10%)
* **Setting:** Modern American Town/City
* **Protagonist:** Hopeless Romantic
* **Antagonist/Supporting:** Rival & Love Interest
* **Theme:** Love Triangle
* **Finale:** Star-Crossed Lovers

## 2. Budgeting & Personnel Management

### 2.1 Production Budgets & Contracting
* **Early Budget Cap:** Aim to keep your early movie budgets strictly between **$150k and $250k**.
* **Contract Types:** Always use **per-movie contracts** for actors, directors, and crew in the early game. Hiring them on long-term monthly salaries introduces a fixed monthly cash burn that will bankrupt you before your first film finishes post-production.

### 2.2 Hiring Department Heads
When hiring executives and department heads:
* **Look for "Cash Upgrade" Traits:** Prioritize executives with cost-saving or cash-generation traits over those with slightly higher base-level stats. A lower-level executive who saves you a percentage on weekly maintenance is infinitely more valuable early on than a high-level executive who drains your reserves.

## 3. The Post-Production Tech Tree

Post-production branches into four specific facility paths. Managing your research and building expansion here is critical to avoiding bankruptcy by maintenance fees.

### 3.1 The Sound Studio Path (Priority #1)
This is your primary growth path. It speeds up post-production turnaround so you can get films into theaters faster, cutting down on daily set and crew maintenance costs.
* **The Route:** *Sound Studio* $\rightarrow$ *Superior Sound Studio* $\rightarrow$ *Fast Track Studio* $\rightarrow$ *Professional Post-Production Facilities*.

### 3.2 Facilities to Avoid Early On
* **Concert Hall:** Useful later for Musicals and Historical Epics (which require booming orchestral scores to maximize Artistic rating), but it carries a brutal ~$2,700+ monthly maintenance cost. Ignore it in Act 1.
* **Film Lab:** Speeds up physical reel processing, but the raw speed isn't necessary on day one. Build it only after your theater monopoly is stable.

## 4. Advertising & Demographic Targeting

Advertising is not a "fire and forget" mechanic. If you market a heavy Drama to teenagers, your attendance will plummet. **Never use more than 3 ad campaigns simultaneously**, as the 4th campaign and beyond suffer from severe diminishing returns.

### 4.1 The Advertising Agency Matrix
Match your agencies strictly to your script's demographics:

* **NBG:** Targets Adult Males & Adult Females. General-purpose commercial coverage.
* **Ross & Ross:** Targets Adult Males & Adult Females. Solid for standard adult commercial runs.
* **Spice Mice:** Targets Teens & Young Adults (Male/Female). **Mandatory** for light comedies and family films. **Avoid at all costs** for heavy Drama/Romance.
* **Velvet Gloss:** Targets Teen, Young Adult, and Adult Females. Weighted by Commercial Score. **Mandatory** for Romance and Female-led Dramas.
* **Viën Pascal:** Targets Young Adults & Adults (Male/Female). **Weighted on Artistic Score**. Use this agency as a bonus when your film has an Artistic Score of 4.0 or higher.
* **Spark:** Targets Young Adults & Adults. Weighted on Commercial Score.
* **Nate Sparrow Press:** Broad coverage for Young Adults & Adults without any specific rating weight.

### 4.2 Free Ad Campaigns (Event Choices)
When prompted with a free campaign choice during early-game events:
* *"For the whole family"* $\rightarrow$ Pick for Comedies and Adventures.
* *"For adults"* $\rightarrow$ Pick for Dramas and Thrillers.
* *"For boys and men"* $\rightarrow$ Pick for Action.

## 5. Theatrical Distribution & Seating Math

Mismanaging theater seating is the #1 reason players lose money on highly-rated films.

### 5.1 The "Total Capacity" Trap (The 55% Attendance Post-Mortem)
If a movie scores well (e.g., 5.5 Commercial / 4.4 Artistic) but only achieves a 55% attendance rate, **you flooded the market**.
Community guides recommending "5,500 to 6,000 seats" for a 5.5-rated film mean **TOTAL SEATS** (Your Studio-Owned Screens + Rented Independent Screens). If you own 3,000 seats and rent 9,500 *more* independent seats, your total capacity hits 12,500. A mid-tier early game release does not have the local population demand to fill 12,500 seats, resulting in empty theaters and massive rental debt.

### 5.2 The Golden Seating Formula
Calculate your target capacity based on your final post-production Commercial Score:
* **Baseline Calculation:** $\text{Commercial Score} \times 1,000 \text{ to } 1,500 = \text{Target Total Capacity}$.
* *(Example: A 5.5 Commercial Score film can support roughly 5,500 to 8,250 total seats on opening week, depending on ad spend).*

### 5.3 The Weekly Tapering Rule
Independent theater rentals cost overhead. You must aggressively taper them down as hype dies:
* **Week 1:** Peak capacity (Target your formula maximum based on Commercial Score and strong ad campaigns).
* **Week 2:** Hold steady or drop capacity by ~20%.
* **Weeks 3–5:** Taper down heavily (cutting 35% to 50% of your rentals each week). By Week 5 or 6, your film should be playing **exclusively** on your studio-owned screens with zero independent rental overhead.

## 6. Crisis Management & PR Scandals

Events will trigger actor scandals, production accidents, or legal troubles.

### 6.1 The "Apologize" Rule
When an actor commits a scandal and you are given the choice to "Do Nothing" or "Apologize" (taking a localized penalty, usually around -60 reputation/score points):
* **Always take the hit and apologize.**
* **Why:** A localized -60 point penalty to a single film or metric hurts briefly, but it acts as a containment measure. Choosing "Do Nothing" allows the scandal to fester, triggering cascading studio-wide penalties. Unmanaged scandals lead to city-wide protests, boycotts, and a permanently tanked studio reputation that will prevent you from securing vital theater distribution contracts in the future.
